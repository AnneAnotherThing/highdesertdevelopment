Before you spend anything

Building on a vacant lot in the High Desert

There is a lot of land out here that was sold decades ago and never built on. If you own a piece of it, this is the order the questions actually come in.

Search online for what it costs to build a house and you get national averages, produced by people who have never stood on caliche in July. Out here the house is often the predictable part. What moves the number is the lot: how far it sits from the last power pole, what happens when you put a shovel in it, and whether the county considers it a legal building site at all.

None of that requires plans, an architect, or a signed contract to find out. Most of it can be answered from a parcel number and an afternoon.

1. Is it a legal, buildable lot?

The first question is not what you want to build, it is whether anything can be built. A parcel needs to be a legally created lot, zoned for a dwelling, and large enough under the local rules for whatever it is going to hold. Some desert parcels sold in the boom years fail one of those tests, and that is worth knowing on day one rather than after the plans are drawn.

Legal access matters just as much. A lot with no recorded access to a maintained road is not a lot you can permit, no matter how easy it looks to drive to.

2. How does water get there?

Two answers, and the difference between them is large.

If there is a water main in the street, this is a connection and a meter. If there is not, the lot needs a well, and a well is its own project: drilling to whatever depth the water sits at in that spot, casing, a pump, a tank and a pressure system. Depth varies enormously across the High Desert, which is why nobody can quote a well honestly without knowing where the lot is.

Drilling wells is work we do ourselves, which means the answer you get is from the people who would be doing it rather than from a subcontractor's guess.

3. How does power get there?

If the poles run past the property, this is a service drop and a panel. If the last pole is a quarter mile back, the utility will quote a line extension, and that number is driven by distance and by whether the run is overhead or trenched.

There is a point where a solar and battery system stops being an environmental preference and starts being the cheaper option outright. Working out where that point sits for your particular lot is worth doing before assuming either way.

4. Will the ground take a septic system?

Away from a sewer main, wastewater is handled on site, and whether that works depends on what the soil does with water. That is established with a percolation test, and the result determines the size and type of system, which in turn determines a meaningful part of the cost.

Rock and caliche are the two words that change the budget here. Both are common, both are workable, and both are much better discovered before the build is priced than during it.

5. What else does the site need?

Land clearing and grading. A driveway or an access road, which on a deep lot can be a real length of work. Trenching for the utility runs once the answers above are known. Sometimes drainage, depending on how the land sits.

This is the stage most people underestimate, because it is invisible in the finished photographs. It is also the stage that makes the rest of the build straightforward or difficult.

6. Then the permits

Which jurisdiction you deal with depends on where the parcel sits. Around here that is usually Kern County, Los Angeles County, San Bernardino County, or one of the incorporated cities inside them. Each has its own submittal requirements, its own timelines, and its own opinions about septic and access.

Permits are part of what we handle. Plans and approvals routinely take longer than people expect, so the useful thing is to start that clock early rather than treat it as a formality at the end.

So what does it cost?

Anyone who gives you a number before seeing the parcel is guessing. What can be said honestly is which factors move it, and the big ones are all above: distance to utilities, depth to water, what the soil does, how much access has to be built, and what the jurisdiction requires.

The useful next step is not a quote. It is finding out whether the lot works, and roughly what it would take to make it ready. That is a conversation, not an invoice, and it is one we are glad to have with somebody who is still deciding.
