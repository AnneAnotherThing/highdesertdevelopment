After a total loss

Rebuilding a home after a fire

If the house is gone, the questions are not the ones a normal building guide answers. This is the order things happen in, and what California already puts on your side before you sign anything.

Most writing about building a house assumes an empty lot and an owner in no particular hurry. Neither is true after a fire. You are working with an insurer, a county, a site that has to be cleared, and a timeline you did not choose.

What follows is what the process actually involves and what to check before you hand anybody a deposit. None of it requires you to have picked a contractor yet.

1. The lot is not starting from zero

A parcel that had a house on it is already an established building site. That is a real advantage over raw land: the utilities were run to it once, the access exists, and the jurisdiction has already accepted that a dwelling belongs there. What has to be established again is the condition of what survived.

The foundation is the first question and the expensive one. Slabs and stem walls sometimes come through a fire and sometimes only look as though they did, because heat changes concrete in ways you cannot see from a walk around the site. That answer comes from an engineer's assessment. Reusing a foundation that should have been replaced is the costliest mistake available at this stage, and it surfaces late.

Below ground, the sewer or septic connection, the water service and the conduit runs all want checking rather than assuming. Underground utility work is what we do most of, and on a rebuild it is usually the part nobody has looked at yet.

2. Clearance comes before design

Nothing gets permitted on a site that has not been cleared and the clearance documented. What that record has to contain, and in what order, is the county's call. Los Angeles County publishes the current requirements for the areas affected by the January 2025 fires at recovery.lacounty.gov ↗.

Read that before paying for design work. The sequence it sets is the one your permit gets judged against, and drawings produced out of order tend to get redrawn.

3. Rebuilding close to what was there is the faster route

Los Angeles County has set up quicker paths for rebuilds that stay close to the footprint, size and design of the house that was lost, alongside sets of pre-approved plans. Redesigning is still allowed. It goes through a longer review.

That is a real decision with a real cost attached, and it is much better made at the start than discovered at plan check. If you have been thinking about a different layout, price the delay alongside the design before you commit to either.

4. The exterior has to meet the wildfire standards

A house rebuilt in a high fire hazard zone has to meet California's wildfire construction standards on everything facing outward: ignition-resistant or noncombustible exterior materials, and vents built to keep embers out.

If you read a guide written before 2026 it will call this Chapter 7A of the building code. Those provisions have been moved into the California Wildland-Urban Interface Code, which is where a plan checker looks now. The requirements are the thing that matters, not the name, but it is worth knowing why two sources appear to disagree.

This is not a detail to leave until the end. It reaches siding, eaves, decking, windows, roofing and every vent in the building, so it belongs in the design rather than in a change order.

5. What the insurer will want to see

An adjuster works from a scope: a written, line-by-line description of what is being rebuilt, with a cost against each part. A contractor's estimate that is one number on one page is hard to reconcile against that, and it is hard for you to check.

Ask for the estimate in the same shape as the adjuster's scope. Where the two differ, the difference becomes a conversation you can actually have instead of a gap somebody absorbs quietly.

6. The contract rules already on your side

California treats rebuilding a home as home improvement work, and home improvement carries rules written for exactly this situation. These are worth knowing before you meet anybody, because they do not depend on the contractor volunteering them.

Your deposit is capped. A contractor cannot ask for more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. Not whichever is more, and there is no exception for special-order materials.

You get seven business days to cancel, not three. The usual right to cancel a home improvement contract is three days. Where a state or local emergency has been declared for the event that damaged the property, that becomes seven business days.

The contract has to state a total price. Time and materials, and cost plus, do not satisfy the law here, because a home improvement contract has to carry a fixed total and a payment schedule in dollars. An open-ended arrangement is not something you have to accept.

Changes go in writing first. Change orders are signed before the work, not settled after it.

The contract also has to name who is pulling the permits, when the work starts and finishes, and the contractor's licence number.

7. Check the licence before you sign

The Contractors State License Board keeps a free lookup at cslb.ca.gov ↗. Search by licence number, business name or a person's name, and it will tell you whether the licence is active, which classifications it holds, whether the bond and workers' compensation are in place, and any complaint history on the record.

It takes about a minute. Do it for everybody who gives you a bid, including us. High Desert Development is licence 771814.
